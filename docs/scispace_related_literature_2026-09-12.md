## 1. Theme 1 — Behavioural Response of Fishing Fleets to MHWs (AIS/VMS)

**(a) Full citation**

Farchadi, N., Welch, H., Braun, C. D., Allyn, A. J., Bograd, S. J., Brodie, S., Hazen, E. L., Kerney, A., Lezama-Ochoa, N., Mills, K. E., Pugh, D., Young-Morse, R., & Lewison, R. L. (2024). Marine heatwaves redistribute pelagic fishing fleets. *Fish and Fisheries*, *25*(4), 602–618. https://doi.org/10.1111/faf.12828

**(b) Method and findings**

Farchadi et al. build ensemble vessel-distribution models (using boosted regression trees and related machine learning methods) for two U.S. pelagic fleets — Atlantic longliners and Pacific albacore trollers — with Global Fishing Watch AIS vessel positions as the response variable and a suite of oceanographic predictors including NOAA OISST sea surface temperature, chlorophyll-*a*, bathymetry, and marine heatwave metrics (cumulative intensity, duration, and spatial extent derived from the Hobday hierarchical definition) as covariates. Separate models by coast allow direct comparison of how MHW characteristics reshape accessible fishing grounds across distinct fleet types and biogeographic settings. The headline finding is that MHW areal size exerts the largest single influence on the contraction of predicted fishing-ground area, exceeding the effects of MHW intensity and duration; displacement patterns differ substantially between the Atlantic longline and Pacific troll fleets, with coastal fleets showing stronger redistribution responses than offshore ones. This constitutes the first systematic, AIS-grounded demonstration that MHWs are a first-order driver of pelagic fleet redistribution across ocean basins, and not merely a trigger for localised avoidance behaviour.

**(c) Connection to the study**

Both studies use AIS-derived fishing activity matched to NOAA OISST and the Hobday MHW classification framework; whereas Farchadi et al. document fleet redistribution at basin scale using distribution models, the Brazilian panel exploits the same inputs in an H3 cell-month fixed-effects design with neighbourhood displacement rings — directly quantifying the distance gradient of the effort response that Farchadi et al. observe in aggregate but do not resolve spatially.

**(d) Journal and tier**

*Fish and Fisheries* (Wiley; ISSN 1467-2960 print / 1467-2979 online) is consistently ranked in the top decile worldwide across the Marine & Freshwater Biology and Fisheries ISI categories and is universally regarded as the leading synthesis journal in fisheries science; it publishes peer-reviewed Reviews, Research Articles, and Perspectives, and is indexed in Web of Science (SCIE) and Scopus. Its citation impact places it in the first tier of the field's specialist outlets.

## 2. Theme 2 — Economic Consequences of MHWs for Fisheries

**(a) Full citation**

Fisher, M. C., Moore, S. K., Jardine, S. L., Watson, J. R., & Samhouri, J. F. (2021). Climate shock effects and mediation in fisheries. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, *118*(2), e2014379117. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2014379117

**(b) Method and findings**

Using the 2015–2016 North Pacific warm anomaly (the "Blob") as an exogenous climate shock, Fisher et al. analyse how the resulting unprecedented closures of multiple U.S. West Coast fisheries disrupted commercial fishing participation across 19 target species in 23 coastal communities. Community economic structure is operationalised as a bipartite participation network — linking individual vessels to the species they target — with portfolio diversity and network centrality metrics computed from licensing and landings records before, during, and after the closure period. The central finding is that fishing communities whose vessels engaged with more complex, diversified species portfolios were substantially less sensitive to the economic disruption induced by closures; fleet-wide participation patterns rebounded to pre-shock states once fisheries were reopened, indicating community-level resilience to this particular perturbation. The paper identifies network complexity as a partial insurance mechanism against climate-shock losses and concludes that management strategies that maintain or expand fishery portfolio diversity enhance community resilience.

**(c) Connection to the study**

Fisher et al.'s natural-experiment design — treating an abrupt, external climate shock as a source of exogenous variation and testing for pre-shock trend equivalence and post-shock rebound — directly parallels the Brazilian study's distributed leads/lags specification, no-anticipation finding, and placebo tests; the Brazilian panel extends this logic to the daily fishing-effort margin in a tropical setting where no formal closures were declared, isolating the spontaneous effort-withdrawal response before institutional interventions occur.

**(d) Journal and tier**

*PNAS* (National Academy of Sciences; ISSN 0027-8424 print / 1091-6490 online) is one of the most-cited multidisciplinary scientific journals in the world, consistently ranked among the global top five across natural and social sciences, and indexed in Web of Science (SCIE), PubMed/MEDLINE, and Scopus; it applies independent member review and stringent statistical scrutiny, making it the highest-prestige citation anchor available for empirical climate–fisheries economics.

## 3. Theme 3 — Fishing Location Choice / Spatial Bioeconomics

**(a) Full citation**

Smith, M. D., Asche, F., Bennear, L. S., & Oglend, A. (2014). Spatial-dynamics of hypoxia and fisheries: The case of Gulf of Mexico brown shrimp. *Marine Resource Economics*, *29*(2), 111–131. https://doi.org/10.1086/676826

**(b) Method and findings**

Smith et al. study how Gulf of Mexico brown shrimp trawlers spatially reallocate effort in response to annually shifting hypoxic dead zones — low-oxygen areas that function as habitat exclusion events structurally parallel to the thermal habitat compression produced by MHWs. Matching fishing trip logbook and effort records to spatially explicit annual hypoxia maps (from LUMCON bottom-water oxygen surveys), the authors estimate a spatial panel bioeconomic model in which fleet effort across latitude-longitude blocks is explained by hypoxia exposure, predicted shrimp biomass, and block-level fixed effects. The key finding is that fishers do not simply absorb the constraint as a catch loss: they systematically displace effort laterally into non-hypoxic adjacent blocks, generating measurable spatial spillovers and heterogeneous zone-level impacts — with vessel capacity moderating the magnitude of displacement. This paper establishes the foundational spatial panel framework for analysing how fishing fleets respond to environmental gradients by reallocating across cells, directly motivating the neighbourhood-ring displacement analysis in the present study.

**(c) Connection to the study**

Smith et al.'s zone-level panel structure — with cell fixed effects absorbing static productivity differences, an environmental shock entering as a time-varying treatment, and displacement inferred from spatial spillovers into neighbouring blocks — maps one-to-one onto the Brazilian study's H3 cell-month design with concentric neighbourhood rings; the positive effort spillover documented at 60–150 km in the Brazilian panel replicates Smith et al.'s core displacement mechanism in a different environmental-shock context (MHW vs. hypoxia) and tropical-Atlantic setting.

**(d) Journal and tier**

*Marine Resource Economics* (University of Chicago Press; ISSN 0738-1360 print / 2334-5985 online) is the oldest dedicated peer-reviewed journal in natural resource and fisheries economics, indexed in Web of Science (SSCI) and Scopus; it is the primary outlet for spatial bioeconomic modelling and econometric analyses of fishery behaviour and is explicitly included on the study's stated list of acceptable journals.

## 4. Theme 4 — South Atlantic / Brazilian Coast: MHW Trends and Drivers

**(a) Full citation**

Rodrigues, R. R., Taschetto, A. S., Sen Gupta, A., & Foltz, G. R. (2019). Common cause for severe droughts in South America and marine heatwaves in the South Atlantic. *Nature Geoscience*, *12*(8), 620–626. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41561-019-0393-8

**(b) Method and findings**

Rodrigues et al. combine NOAA OISST sea surface temperature records, ERA-Interim atmospheric reanalysis, GPCP precipitation fields, OSCAR surface-current data, and satellite-derived shortwave radiation to investigate whether South Atlantic MHWs and South American droughts share a common atmospheric origin. Composite and singular-value-decomposition analyses reveal that anomalous anticyclonic blocking over the South Atlantic — driven by Rossby wave trains linked to Pacific SST variability via the Pacific–South American teleconnection — simultaneously suppresses latent heat loss at the ocean surface (warming it beyond the MHW threshold) and diverts moisture transport away from Brazil and neighbouring countries (producing drought). The 2014–2015 event — which yielded record droughts in São Paulo concurrent with a pronounced South Atlantic MHW off the Brazilian coast — is shown to be mechanistically representative rather than exceptional. The paper establishes South Atlantic MHWs as a physically coherent, regionally recurrent phenomenon with an identified atmospheric forcing mechanism, not solely a passive local response to secular ocean warming.

**(c) Connection to the study**

Rodrigues et al. provide the physical oceanographic foundation for the study's setting: the atmospheric blocking mechanism they document generates the anomalously warm SST events in NOAA OISST v2.1 that the Hobday algorithm classifies as MHWs in the Brazilian cell-month panel, and their analysis of the 2014–2015 event falls within the study's 2013–2024 observation window — situating the identified fishing-effort effects within a characterised, recurring regional climate process operating on exactly the Brazilian coastal ocean.

**(d) Journal and tier**

*Nature Geoscience* (Springer Nature; ISSN 1752-0894 print / 1752-0908 online) is one of the Nature family's flagship earth-science journals, ranked among the top one or two journals worldwide in the Geosciences/Multidisciplinary ISI category with a 2023 Impact Factor exceeding 18; it applies the same structured peer-review standard as *Nature* itself and is indexed in Web of Science (SCIE) and Scopus — the pre-eminent venue for high-impact observational and mechanistic ocean/atmosphere science.

## 5. Theme 5 — AIS Data Quality, Coverage Bias, and Inference Limits

**(a) Full citation**

Hintzen, N. T., Brigden, K., Kaastra, H.-J., Mackinson, S., Pastoors, M. A., & van de Pol, L. (2025). Bias in Global Fishing Watch AIS data analyses results in overestimate of Northeast Atlantic pelagic fishing impact. *ICES Journal of Marine Science*, *82*(3), fsaf033. https://doi.org/10.1093/icesjms/fsaf033

**(b) Method and findings**

Hintzen et al. design a direct validation study by cross-referencing GFW-derived fishing-event classifications against self-sampling observer records from two large pelagic fleets — Dutch pelagic freezer trawlers and Scottish purse seiners — operating in the Northeast Atlantic, where the exact position and start/end time of every haul were independently recorded on board. Matching these ground-truth records to GFW classified events shows that GFW overestimates gear-deployment duration by 30%–380%, with the magnitude of overestimation varying by target species and vessel type. The authors identify the primary mechanism driving the bias: low vessel speeds during active acoustic prospecting (sonar and echosounder use to locate fish schools) are algorithmically indistinguishable from gear deployment in GFW's speed-threshold classifiers, causing search time to be recorded as fishing time. Cross-validation against unprocessed VMS data confirms the bias originates in GFW's classification assumptions rather than in the underlying AIS transmission record; the authors recommend that fisheries researchers obtain access to raw AIS positions or engage GFW in collaborative algorithm development with the fishing sector.

**(c) Connection to the study**

The study's dependent variable — GFW apparent fishing hours — inherits the classification bias Hintzen et al. document: the PPML estimates capture the MHW effect on inferred fishing activity, which may compress both genuine gear-in-water time and vessel search behaviour recorded as fishing hours; the cell fixed effects absorb time-invariant misclassification patterns, and the gear-type heterogeneity analysis indirectly addresses the vessel-type-specific bias magnitudes that Hintzen et al. show vary across fleet segments — a caveat that warrants explicit acknowledgment in the study's empirical-design discussion.

**(d) Journal and tier**

*ICES Journal of Marine Science* (Oxford University Press / International Council for the Exploration of the Sea; ISSN 1054-3139 print / 1095-9289 online) is the oldest continuously published fisheries and ocean-science journal, ranked among the top journals in the Marine & Freshwater Biology and Fisheries ISI categories; it is indexed in Web of Science (SCIE) and Scopus and is the benchmark empirical outlet for AIS/VMS data methodology, fisheries monitoring, and stock assessment in fisheries science.

## Literature Narrative (≤150 words)

Rodrigues et al. (2019) establish that South Atlantic MHWs arise from regionally specific atmospheric blocking, providing the physical context for the SST anomalies classified in the panel. Smith et al. (2014) supply the spatial bioeconomic template — a panel model showing that environmental shocks displace fishing effort into adjacent cells rather than reducing it uniformly — while Fisher et al. (2021) document community-level economic costs when climate shocks trigger institutional fishery closures, demonstrating heterogeneous sensitivity across fleet portfolios. Farchadi et al. (2024) show that AIS-measured fleet redistribution during MHWs is empirically tractable at basin scale; Hintzen et al. (2025) calibrate the limits of that AIS signal by quantifying GFW classification bias. Together these five works expose a clear gap: no study has applied high-frequency AIS panel methods with neighbourhood displacement rings to a tropical fleet operating under discontinued national fisheries statistics — the vacuum the present study fills.
